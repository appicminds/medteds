What 10,000 ECGs Taught Me About Listening

The most important diagnostic tool in the clinic isn't the machine. It's the five minutes before you order the test.

AR
Dr. Amara Reyes Follow
Published in Heart Matters · 8 min read · Nov 4, 2026

Illustration by the author

In my first year as a cardiology fellow, I believed the twelve-lead tracing would tell me everything. The waveform was objective. The patient's story was noise I had to sit through before getting to the signal.

Ten thousand tracings later, I've come to think almost exactly the opposite.

The test answers the question you asked

An ECG is a remarkably honest instrument. It shows you the electrical activity of the heart during a ten-second window, and nothing more. If the arrhythmia happens at 3 a.m. while climbing stairs, the tracing taken at 2 p.m. in a quiet room will be normal — and normal can be the most dangerous word in medicine.

“A normal result is only reassuring if you asked the right question.”

The patient who mentions, almost in passing, that she gets lightheaded “only when I carry laundry upstairs” has given you more diagnostic information than the machine will. The question is whether you were listening when she said it.

Five minutes, three questions

These days, before I look at any tracing, I ask three things: When does it happen? What were you doing? What made it stop? The answers shape which test I order, how long I monitor, and what I'm looking for when the results come back.

Medicine is a conversation that occasionally pauses for a lab result.

None of this is new. Every attending I trained under said some version of it. But it took a few thousand tracings — and a few humbling misses — before I really believed it.
